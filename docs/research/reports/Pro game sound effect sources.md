# Three packs beat AI for $239

The cheapest, safest route to professional, human-made, cartoon-quality sound effects that Rascal Rally! can legally ship as MP3 files from GitHub Pages is three one-time pack purchases totaling roughly **$239** — not a subscription, not an AI generator, and not a freelancer. **Shapeforms Audio's "Complete Collection 2025"** ($149, marked down from $434) bundles 7,500+ sounds across 15 sub-libraries — a 490-file impact library, a 450-file coins/pickup library, an 800-file UI-chime library, and, matching this project's own "pickups are balloons, not boxes" rule, a dedicated 342-recording balloon library — covering four of the six requested sound groups in one checkout ([Shapeforms Complete Collection](https://www.shapeforms.com/shop/p/complete-collection-2024)). **Silverplatter Audio's "Go Karts"** pack (~$27 CAD, 24 real go-kart recordings) supplies engine loops and revs, and **Sonniss's "Skid and Drift"** pack ($70, 48 files) supplies purpose-recorded go-kart and drift-surface tire squeal ([Silverplatter Go Karts](https://silverplatteraudio.com/products/go-karts); [Sonniss Skid and Drift](https://sonniss.com/sound-effects/skid-and-drift/)). Every one-time vendor researched permits synchronized, in-game use without attribution; the sharpest legal traps sit on the subscription side, where **Artlist requires a custom-quoted Enterprise plan for any game use at all**, and **Adobe Stock's game-eligible Extended License isn't purchasable below Adobe's own Enterprise tier**, disqualifying both regardless of their advertised consumer prices ([Artlist Enterprise plan](https://help.artlist.io/hc/en-us/articles/33980035053213-Enterprise-plan-explained); [Adobe Stock FAQ](https://helpx.adobe.com/stock/help/usage-licensing.html)). AI generation was excluded on Adam's own standing rejection of the game's ElevenLabs audio, and the research backs that call rather than undercuts it: the sole "AI SFX quality leader" claim found traces to a generic tool-roundup blog, not a specialist game-audio source, and nothing anywhere claims AI convincingly fakes a multi-RPM vehicle engine — exactly the hardest sound on the list, and the one real recordings solve outright. One category, boost and whoosh effects, has no confirmed purpose-built pack at any vendor checked and should be mined from the already-free Sonniss GDC bundle and the already-approved Kenney packs before any new money is spent. All three recommended purchases are instant digital downloads, require no attribution, and total well under 6 GB of the 15 GB free-disk budget — buyable and usable in an afternoon, days ahead of 29 September.

## One-time packs carry the cleanest license text of any option

Nearly every professional sound-effects vendor researched — Sonniss, BOOM Library, Sound Ideas, GameDev Market, Fab, the Unity Asset Store — licenses its catalog under the same decades-old **synchronization pattern**: sound effects can be used inside a shipped production (game, film, website, app) royalty-free and typically without attribution, but the raw files can never be resold, sublicensed, or handed to end users as standalone, extractable audio ([BOOM Library terms](https://www.boomlibrary.com/terms-conditions-regular/); [Sound Ideas EULA](https://sound-ideas.com/pages/sound-ideas-end-user-license-agreement)). Several vendors' actual legal text — not just marketing copy — names "websites" and "video games" side by side as explicitly permitted uses: BOOM Library's EULA lists "websites... video games" in the same sentence, Sound Ideas' Rights Granted clause separately enumerates "Web pages & multimedia presentations" and "interactive programs & computer games," and Ovani Sound's terms class games as "interactive media," distinct from the soundboards it prohibits ([BOOM Library terms](https://www.boomlibrary.com/terms-conditions-regular/); [Sound Ideas EULA](https://sound-ideas.com/pages/sound-ideas-end-user-license-agreement); [Ovani terms of service](https://ovanisound.com/policies/terms-of-service)). Among the broader multi-seller marketplaces, Fab's EULA is the most emphatic that engine choice is irrelevant ("usage is not limited to Unreal Engine"), GameDev Market's licence defines "Media Product" to explicitly include websites, and AudioJungle's SFX license covers "apps, games... websites" but scopes each purchase to one game rather than unlimited reuse; Unity's own EULA text is engine-agnostic too, though its developer community is visibly split on whether that holds in practice, and individual "Restricted Assets" can carry narrower seller-set terms worth checking per listing ([Fab EULA](https://www.fab.com/eula?lang=en); [GameDev Market terms](https://www.gamedevmarket.net/terms-conditions); [AudioJungle SFX license](https://themeforest.net/licenses/terms/audio_sfx_media_single); [Unity Asset Store EULA FAQ](https://assetstore.unity.com/browse/eula-faq)). The one vendor whose EULA is a genuine outlier is **Pro Sound Effects**, which states plainly that "if You Copy or Broadcast any End User Work on Your website(s), You shall not make any of the Licensed Audio Content on Your website(s) available as a standalone file... without obtaining additional authorization" — despite selling a perfectly-named $5 "Go Kart Engine Start" clip, PSE is the one name to treat with real caution here. Epic Stock Media and SoundMorph each carry a narrower version of the same worry — Epic Stock Media requires a paid Custom Application License for any "web app where your end users can control playback," and SoundMorph separately warns that files "cannot be stored or shared on a server without a multiuser license" — both are worth a one-line clarifying email before relying on, though neither disqualifies the vendor since Rascal Rally triggers sounds from gameplay events, not user-selected playback ([Epic Stock Media licensing](https://epicstockmedia.com/licensing-agreement/)).

On AI provenance, only **Ovani Sound** makes an explicit, checkable claim: its homepage states "100% HUMAN MADE PRODUCTS – NO AI," and its terms separately forbid using Ovani content to train AI systems ([Ovani terms of service](https://ovanisound.com/policies/terms-of-service)). Every other vendor's EULA bans using *their* sounds to train AI models — a policy protecting their catalog, not a disclosure about how it was made — leaving "not AI-generated" an inference from reputation rather than a stated fact for Sonniss, BOOM Library, Sound Ideas, Epic Stock Media, and SoundMorph alike. Fab is the one marketplace with a structural safeguard: sellers must tag any AI-made asset "CreatedWithAI," a label a buyer can check per listing before paying ([Fab EULA](https://www.fab.com/eula?lang=en)). Mapped against Rascal Rally's actual sound list, the deepest single hit is Shapeforms' Complete Collection (detailed in the final section below); for engine and drift material specifically, no per-pack vendor sells a purpose-built "go-kart, multiple RPMs" loop set, but **Silverplatter Audio's "Go Karts"** (24 stereo WAV files, 364 MB, 96 kHz/24-bit, capturing idles, starts, stops, screeches and pass-bys of "high and medium performance go karts") and **Sonniss's "Skid and Drift"** (48 files, 4.65 GB, tire screech/slide/scuff recorded from "drift cars, go-karts, motocross bikes, bicycles, skateboards, ice skates, and rollerblades") are the only two libraries found anywhere in this research that name go-karts specifically among their recorded sources, rather than generic passenger-car or truck engines ([Silverplatter Go Karts](https://silverplatteraudio.com/products/go-karts); [Sonniss Skid and Drift](https://sonniss.com/sound-effects/skid-and-drift/)). Cheaper, generic-car itch.io packs exist too — "Common Gas Cars Sounds" ($5, 150 sounds) and "Car Engines Sound Pack Vol. 1/2" ($25/$30) — but they license per-listing with no platform-wide guarantee, and their source vehicles are ordinary cars, not the higher-pitched small-engine character a go-kart actually has. Two of the six requested categories came up empty across every vendor checked: no library anywhere in this research is sold under a "boost/mini-turbo whoosh" or "jump hop and landing" name — a genuine catalog gap, not a research shortfall.

## Artlist and Adobe Stock wall games out entirely

Subscription libraries look attractive on price until their fine print is read against "ships inside a free public game" specifically. Two of the biggest brands fail outright. **Artlist's consumer "Music & SFX" plans ($9.99–$24.92/month) explicitly exclude games**, and the only tier that permits game use — a custom-quoted Enterprise contract, benchmarked by one aggregator at roughly $24,611 a year — is neither price-competitive nor closeable in days ([Artlist Enterprise plan](https://help.artlist.io/hc/en-us/articles/33980035053213-Enterprise-plan-explained); [Photutorial: Artlist pricing](https://photutorial.com/artlist-pricing/)). **Adobe Stock is worse: the Extended License its own FAQ says is required for "apps, games" is not purchasable at all below Adobe's Enterprise tier**, meaning an ordinary Individual or Team subscriber literally cannot license Adobe Stock audio for any game, at any price ([Adobe Stock FAQ](https://helpx.adobe.com/stock/help/usage-licensing.html)). Motion Array's own license page never lists "games" among its covered uses either, and given it is now Artlist-owned, that is a reasonable amber flag worth a confirmation email rather than a purchase ([Motion Array License](https://motionarray.com/license/)).

By contrast, **Zapsplat, Soundsnap, Soundly, Storyblocks, and Splice all explicitly clear games** in their primary license text, with sounds downloaded during an active subscription staying licensed for that project for life even after cancellation ([Zapsplat Standard License](https://www.zapsplat.com/license-type/standard-license/); [Soundsnap License](https://www.soundsnap.com/license); [Soundly FAQ](https://getsoundly.com/faq/can-i-use-the-sounds-in-a-video-game/); [Storyblocks License Comparison](https://www.storyblocks.com/business-solutions/license-comparison)). Zapsplat's category tree is the closest catalog match to this project's actual needs — dedicated "Cartoon," "Vehicles > Car," "Sport > Motor Racing," "Sound Design > Whoosh," and "Multimedia and UI" buckets all exist as first-class categories in one library — and its Gold tier is framed as a pay-what-you-want donation, reported as low as £4-5/month though not independently confirmed in USD, meaning a single subscribed month spent manually pulling WAVs across those categories could plausibly be the cheapest dollar-for-dollar route in this entire research, at the cost of trading curated speed for hands-on search time ([Zapsplat Standard License](https://www.zapsplat.com/license-type/standard-license/)). Download ceilings vary widely on the subscription side and are worth weighing against that same trade: Splice charges by credit (100/month on its cheapest $9.99 tier, one credit per sound), Soundsnap's plans reportedly range from 150 downloads/month to fully unlimited depending on tier, and Zapsplat's Gold tier claims "unlimited... subject to our Fair Use Policy" without ever publishing a numeric ceiling — every subscription option also requires keeping a live account and payment method active for as long as a file might need re-downloading, where a one-time pack purchase needs nothing but a single checkout ([Splice plans](https://melodyflare.com/splice-sounds-price/)). Envato Elements is a workable but noisier fit: its license is granted per download, requiring each new "End Product" to separately register the same Item, and its clause 12(e) — "you must not permit an end user to extract an Item and use it separately from the End Product" — is the single strictest no-extraction wording found anywhere in this research, worth flagging even though a browser game's asset folder is arguably not what that clause was written to police ([Envato Elements License](https://help.elements.envato.com/hc/en-us/articles/360000628966-Envato-Elements-License)). None of these five games-friendly services' terms use the words "browser game," "HTML5," or "WebGL" anywhere found, and none appear to have been drafted with the literal mechanics of a modern web page in mind.

## Real go-kart recordings beat plugins and AI on engine sound

The professional playbook for convincing vehicle-engine audio — confirmed by a Designing Sound feature on Project CARS and Forza Motorsport 5, and by BOOM Library's own "Car Engine Sound Primer" — is to crossfade loops sampled at regular RPM intervals per microphone perspective, driven by whatever RPM value the game's own physics produces. BOOM Library's primer gives the concrete numbers: roughly **13 loops per engine perspective**, spaced every 500 RPM through the rev range and every 250 RPM at the low end, with separate on-throttle and off-throttle variants for each ([Designing Sound: Vehicle Engine Design](https://designingsound.org/2014/08/11/vehicle-engine-design-project-cars-forza-motorsport-5-and-rev/); [BOOM Library: Car Engine Sound Primer](https://www.boomlibrary.com/blog/the-car-engine-sound-primer-mike-caviezel/)). Veteran designer Greg Hill frames this "loop-based" method as the practical alternative to real-time granular synthesis, which needs only three source files (an acceleration ramp, a deceleration ramp, and an idle loop) but demands specialized runtime tooling — same source. That runtime tooling, **Crankcase Audio's REV**, ships as a plugin for Wwise, FMOD, and Unreal Engine with no browser or Web Audio integration of any kind, ruling it out for a Three.js game outright ([Crankcase Audio](https://www.crankcaseaudio.com/)). **Krotos Igniter** ($399–$599) and **Krotos Studio Pro** (reported $196–$280/year) sit in the same position: useful only as an authoring step — perform the sound, bounce to WAV, encode to MP3 yourself — since neither vendor states a license for redistributing the rendered audio inside a shipped game, paid or free ([Krotos Igniter](https://www.krotosaudio.com/igniter/); [Krotos Studio car driving SFX](https://krotos.studio/blog/car-driving-sfx)). Buying real recordings and building the crossfade ladder by hand is therefore not a compromise for this project's stack — with no Wwise, FMOD, Unity, or Unreal runtime in Rascal Rally!, it is close to the only practical path, and it is exactly what Silverplatter's 24-file Go Karts pack and Sonniss's Skid and Drift pack supply raw material for.

AI sound generation was excluded from this research's shopping list on Adam's own standing instruction, and the findings back that call rather than undercut it. Every 2026 text-to-SFX tool surveyed — **Stable Audio** ($12–$199/month, or $0.26 per generation via API), **Adobe Firefly** ("Generate Sound Effects," four variations up to 30 seconds per prompt), and **ElevenLabs** (40 credits per second, roughly $0.03–$0.08 per generated effect on paid tiers) — offers a workable commercial license for baked-in game use, so licensing is not the problem ([Stable Audio Pricing](https://stableaudio.com/pricing); [Adobe Firefly sound effects](https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2025/07/17/firefly-adds-new-video-capabilities-industry-leading-ai-models-generate-sound-effects-feature); [ElevenLabs Sound Effects docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/capabilities/sound-effects)). The problem is quality for exactly this use case: the only source claiming "ElevenLabs SFX is the current quality leader" is a generic AI-tool-roundup blog, not a specialist game-audio publication, and it sits in direct, unresolved tension with Adam's own hands-on verdict that the ElevenLabs audio already in the game was cheap and terrible ([getimg.ai: Best AI Sound Effect Generator 2026](https://getimg.ai/blog/best-ai-sound-effect-generator)). No source found anywhere in this research — vendor, reviewer, or paper — specifically claims any generator convincingly fakes a multi-RPM vehicle engine, precisely the hardest and most exposed sound on this project's whole list. Meta's AudioGen/AudioCraft weights are non-commercial-only (CC-BY-NC 4.0) and would need local GPU hosting to even try, ruling them out on licensing grounds before quality even enters the picture; Freesound's own API is likewise free only for non-commercial use, with commercial terms negotiated case-by-case; and two research-grade generators, TangoFlux and MMAudio, either lack a hosted commercial offering entirely or are built to dub sound onto existing video rather than generate an isolated game SFX from a text prompt — none of the three is a realistic option on this deadline regardless of quality ([facebookresearch/audiocraft](https://github.com/facebookresearch/audiocraft); [Freesound API Terms of Use](https://freesound.org/docs/api/terms_of_use.html); [MMAudio](https://mmaudio.net/sound-effects)). Real go-kart recordings sidestep this problem entirely rather than gambling on it a second time.

## Freelance hire is the priciest, riskiest path to 29 September

Commissioning 40–80 bespoke cartoon-racing sounds from a freelancer was researched as a fallback, and the numbers argue against it as the primary route under this deadline. **Fiverr** is the only channel combining a large, tagged pool of "game sound design" specialists, a paid rush-delivery mechanic, and visible ratings — one "Fiverr's Choice" seller carries 1,152 reviews at 5.0 stars specifically tagged "Game sound design" — while **Upwork's** own two hire-page variants disagree with each other on price ($500–$1,500 vs. $500–$1,200 per project), and its open job board skews toward $5–20/hour postings per a r/GameAudio thread, making it noisier to budget against ([Fiverr: Hire Sound Effects Experts](https://www.fiverr.com/hire/sound-effects); [Upwork: Game Audio Designer](https://www.upwork.com/hire/game-audio-designer/); [r/GameAudio](https://www.reddit.com/r/GameAudio/comments/1t1fug1/how_do_sound_designers_actually_make_a_living_on_reddit/)). Blending a forum-sourced benchmark of **$30–50 per individually crafted sound effect** with Fiverr's own category average of **$104–236 per gig** (which typically bundles several sounds already) puts a realistic 40–80-sound order at roughly **$300 to $4,000** before any rush surcharge, and rush delivery commonly adds another **25–100%** on top — a wide, hard-to-pin-down range against a fixed, far cheaper pack-buying alternative ([VI-Control: SFX and Sound Design Fees](https://vi-control.net/community/threads/sfx-and-sound-design-fees.15470/); [Fiverr video games sound design](https://www.fiverr.com/categories/music-audio/buy/sound-design/video-games); [A Sound Effect: freelance rates](https://www.asoundeffect.com/freelance-sound-rates/)). Rights default close to safe — both platforms assign full copyright to the buyer on payment — but each carries a gotcha worth checking first: a Fiverr gig can carve out a paid "Commercial Use License" upsell that a buyer must actively confirm covers "integration into products," and Upwork excludes the freelancer's own pre-existing "Background Technology" from the transfer ([Fiverr Terms of Service](https://www.fiverr.com/legal-portal/legal-terms/terms-of-service); [Upwork ownership terms](https://terms.law/2023/07/23/upworks-terms-ownership-of-freelancers-work-product/)). Most relevant given this project has already rejected one batch of AI-made audio: **Fiverr's own help center publishes an explicit AI-disclosure policy** because AI-generated work passed off as original is an acknowledged, active problem on the platform — documented so far mostly in music and vocal gigs rather than SFX specifically, but a real, citable reason to state "no AI-generated audio, dry stems available on request" directly in any brief, and to commission one quick paid sample before committing to a full order ([Fiverr: Using AI on Fiverr](https://help.fiverr.com/hc/en-us/articles/34998793899665-Using-AI-on-Fiverr-Guidelines-for-freelancers-and-clients)). No seller portfolio found in this research showed prior cartoon-kart-racing work specifically, so freelance hire is best reserved as a narrow patch for one hard-to-source sound — a bespoke boost whoosh, say — rather than the primary route for the whole list.

## Conclusion

The deeper pattern here is that "cheapest" and "safest" turned out to be the same answer, not a trade-off: the two subscription brands with the broadest name recognition, Artlist and Adobe Stock, are also the two that quietly cannot cover a shipped game at any self-serve price, while the smallest, least-famous vendors — a Canadian go-kart recordist, a boutique cartoon-SFX shop, a one-catalog library seller — turned out to have both the cleanest license text and the lowest prices. The second takeaway is architectural rather than commercial: not one license, subscription or one-time, among the dozens read in this research was drafted with the literal mechanics of a browser game in mind, where every sound necessarily lives at its own fetchable public URL that a curious visitor's dev tools can see. That gap cannot be shopped away by picking a different vendor; it has to be closed in the codebase, by never exposing a browsable sounds folder or a "download our SFX" page and loading every clip only through the game's own code — the same behavior every "no standalone extraction" clause across every vendor is actually aimed at stopping. Whichever packs Adam buys, that one implementation detail matters more than which vendor's logo ends up on the invoice.
